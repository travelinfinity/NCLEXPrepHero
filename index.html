A client's potassium is 6.9 mEq/L and the monitor shows tall, peaked T waves. Which order should the nurse carry out first?

15-Second Memory Trick ("C before I"): Peaked T-waves mean the myocardium is irritable and about to arrest. Calcium gluconate (B) puts a protective shield over the heart muscle in 1–3 minutes. Once the heart is safe, give Regular Insulin + Dextrose (C) to push potassium into the cells, and Kayexalate (A) ("K-Exits-Late") to excrete it in stool!

🚨 6. The 5 Deadly 'D' Labs (Stay at Bedside!)

  • 1. Potassium > 6.0 mEq/L: Deadly cardiac arrest risk. Hold K+, attach monitor, give IV Calcium Gluconate FIRST (protects the heart), then Regular Insulin + Dextrose, then remove K+ (binders/dialysis).
  • 2. Arterial pH in the 6.0s (< 7.00): Lethal acidemia. Assess vitals, keep Ambu-bag at bedside, call HCP STAT.
  • 3. PaCO2 in the 60s (≥ 60 mmHg): Acute hypercapnic respiratory failure. Prepare for immediate intubation & mechanical ventilation.
  • 4. PaO2 in the 60s (≤ 60 mmHg): Severe hypoxemic respiratory failure. Apply high-flow O2, prepare to intubate.
  • 5. Platelets < 40,000 /mm³: Spontaneous fatal hemorrhage risk. Strict bleeding precautions & prepare platelet transfusion.

It's adaptive: get one right and the next gets harder. Feeling like every question is brutal usually means you're doing well. A long exam does not mean you're failing, and a short one doesn't guarantee a pass.

The 3-step stem scan 🔍

Before you even look at the options, interrogate the question like a TV detective with a dramatic coat.

1

Who is this client?

Age, diagnosis and setting tell you what's normal for them. A heart rate of 140 is a crisis at 70 years old and a Tuesday at 7 months.

2

What are they really asking?

First / priority = only one thing happens first. Needs further teaching = hunt for the wrong statement. Expected finding = boring is correct.

3

Find the plot twist

One small phrase changes everything: suddenly, new, not relieved by rest, 2 hours after surgery. The diagnosis is the setting; the twist is the story.

The priority ladder 🪜

Climb from the top. The first rung that separates your clients wins.

  1. Is anyone dying right now?Airway → Breathing → Circulation. Also: major hemorrhage, a blood glucose in the basement, active seizures, no pulse.
  2. New or changing beats expected and steadyA fever that just spiked outranks a fever that's been there all week with the same cause.
  3. Unstable beats stableSee the red flags vs calm signals below.
  4. Fresh beats familiarJust out of surgery, just admitted, just diagnosed, or just changed. Newer clients are needier clients.
  5. Still a tie? Follow the organ that fails fastestBrain → lungs → heart → the rest. Judge the organ in the finding, not the diagnosis on the chart.

🚩 Red flags (go now)

  • Sudden change in level of consciousness
  • New shortness of breath, stridor or SpO₂ dropping
  • Pain unrelieved by the usual treatment
  • Unexpected findings for the diagnosis
  • Critical labs: K⁺ in the 6s, pH under 7.2, platelets very low
  • Under 12 hours after general anesthesia

🟢 Calm signals (can wait a moment)

  • Chronic condition, unchanged from baseline
  • Findings that are expected for the diagnosis
  • Being discharged or awaiting discharge
  • Mildly abnormal labs that match the condition
  • Stable vital signs with a known trend
  • Local anesthesia, procedure finished hours ago

Assess or act? If the stem gives you no data, assess first. If it hands you the data and the client is crashing, act. You don't need another set of vitals to know a client without a pulse needs compressions.

Delegation: who does what 👥

The RN keeps the TAPE: Teach, Assess, Plan, Evaluate. Everything else depends on how stable and predictable the client is.

RN

  • Admission, first and changed-condition assessments
  • Initial teaching and discharge teaching
  • Care planning and evaluating outcomes
  • Unstable or unpredictable clients
  • IV push meds, blood products, central lines

LPN / LVN

  • Stable clients with predictable outcomes
  • Reinforcing teaching the RN started
  • Routine meds (per state scope), dressing changes on established wounds
  • Data collection (the RN interprets it)

UAP / AIN

  • Hygiene, feeding stable clients, toileting
  • Vital signs on stable clients
  • Ambulation after the client has walked once with the RN
  • Measuring and recording intake and output

Rule of firsts: the first time anything happens (first assessment, first walk after surgery, first tube feeding) belongs to the RN. Families can be taught specific tasks, but safety is never handed over. If a parent wants the crib rail down, you stay until it's back up.

Staff drama decision tree 🎭

Is someone in danger right now?Unsafe care in progress → step in immediately and take over the task.
Is it illegal?Drug diversion, falsified records → report up the chain of command (charge nurse or supervisor).
Just unprofessional?Rudeness, sloppy habits → a private conversation later.
"Ignore it"Never the right answer. Not even if the coworker brings donuts.

Smart guessing (last resort only) 🎲

☂️ The umbrella

If several answers are true, pick the one that covers the others, often the safety answer.

👯 Twins are out

Two options that say the same thing in different words can't both be right, so usually neither is.

🚫 Absolutes are suspicious

"Always" and "never" are rarely true in nursing. Except "never push potassium". That one is real.

💬 Feelings first

In communication questions, choose the reply that explores the client's feelings. Avoid "why", false reassurance and changing the subject.

📋 Data given? Act.

If the stem already hands you the assessment, the answer is usually an action, not another assessment.

🔁 Stuck between two?

Reread the stem. The plot twist you missed is usually hiding in plain sight.

Quick concept capsules 💊

Acid–base in 5 seconds

Look at pH first (low = acidosis, high = alkalosis). If pH and HCO₃⁻ arrows point the same way, it's metabolic. Opposite ways? Respiratory.

Ventilator alarms

High pressure = something's in the way: kinks, water in the tubing, secretions. Low pressure = something's loose: a disconnection or leak. Can't fix it fast? Bag the client.

Lab traffic lights

🟢 Note it. 🟡 Tell the provider soon. 🔴 Call now and stay at the bedside: K⁺ over 6, very low pH, very low platelets, SpO₂ falling fast.
